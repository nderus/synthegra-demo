How to read this
C-index
Take any two patients. Does the model correctly say who has the event first? 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect. Real prognostic models sit between 0.6 and 0.85.
Best single layer
The better of the two layers used alone. Fusion has to beat this to be worth its cost.
Late fusion (vote)
Each specialist votes: one model per layer, average the risk scores. Cannot see interactions between layers.
Early fusion, linear
Pile every report on one desk and fit a Cox model: every feature adds its own risk, nothing multiplies.
Early fusion, non-linear
Same desk, but a model (boosted survival trees) that can learn "this mutation matters only when that pathway is high".
Intermediate fusion (best neural)
Each layer gets its own neural encoder, the encodings are fused, one survival head on top. Synthegra has five fusion architectures; for every cohort the best one by validation C-index is shown.
Oracle
The C-index you would get if you knew the true risk of every simulated patient. The ceiling for this cohort. Not defined for the IBS.
IBS
Integrated Brier score: the average squared error of the predicted survival probability over follow-up, with censoring weights. It rewards both ranking and calibration. 0 is perfect; predicting the population curve for everyone gives roughly 0.15–0.25 depending on the event rate.
Shared · unique · interaction
Two witnesses to the same event. Shared: both tell the same story. Unique: only one saw it. Interaction: the clue appears only when the two stories are combined (synergy).

Why this matters

If the band of the information read-out touches zero at your cohort size, a null result on synergy is uninformative: the instrument cannot resolve it. The prediction read-out asks the practical question: can a model turn that synergy into better risk ranking? Its linear line is the control: a linear model cannot use interactions, so any gain it shows comes from the second layer's unique information, not from synergy. The neural intermediate fusion is the model built to use interactions; its advantage appears only past a certain cohort size, and that size is the practical resolution limit. The paper-sweep option shows the same two read-outs on a harsher structure (many latent factors, dominant interaction terms) where nothing unlocks below a few thousand patients.

Does synthetic survival look real?

The generator is calibrated so that synthetic cohorts reproduce the survival distribution of a real myeloid-neoplasm cohort. This is a calibration check, not a claim that synthetic data replaces real patients.

p = 0.91log-rank, real vs synthetic

Kaplan-Meier curves of a real MDS/AML cohort and a synthetic cohort generated with the same censoring mechanism and baseline hazard. Shaded bands are 95% confidence intervals.

Synthetic cohorts are for benchmarking methods, not for clinical inference or for sharing in place of patient data.
